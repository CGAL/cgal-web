General Information

What is CGAL?

CGAL, the Computational Geometry Algorithms Library, is an open source C++ library that provides easy access to efficient and reliable geometric algorithms and data structures to users in industry and academia. CGAL is also the short name of the CGAL Open Source Project, which was founded in 1996 and develops the library.

A distinctive feature of CGAL is its focus on robustness: its algorithms produce correct results even on degenerate inputs and despite the limited precision of floating-point arithmetic (see exact computation). CGAL is written in generic C++ and can be used with your own data structures (see concepts and models).

What can I do with CGAL?

CGAL offers more than one hundred packages, among which:

  • 2D and 3D triangulations (Delaunay, constrained, regular, periodic), Voronoi diagrams, and alpha shapes;
  • surface and volume mesh generation, remeshing, and mesh optimization;
  • polygon mesh processing: Boolean operations, repair, hole filling, smoothing, simplification, distance computations;
  • 2D polygons: Boolean operations, offsetting, straight skeletons, Minkowski sums, partitioning, repair;
  • arrangements of curves and their applications;
  • point set processing and shape reconstruction from point clouds;
  • convex hulls, spatial searching (AABB trees, kd-trees), geometric optimization, and interpolation.

The Package Overview lists all packages with a short description and a picture.

Who develops CGAL?

CGAL is developed by a community of developers working at research institutes, universities, and companies. The project is steered by the CGAL Editorial Board. See the pages on the people involved, the partners and funding sources, and the rules of the project.

How do you pronounce CGAL?

CGAL is pronounced like "seagull" in English, or "cigale" in French.

How did you make the CGAL logo?

See this page for information about the construction of the CGAL logo.

What is CGAL used for?

CGAL is used wherever reliable geometric computations are needed, in commercial software, open source projects, and research. Typical application domains are computer aided design and manufacturing, 3D printing (slicing, Boolean operations, and mesh repair), geographic information systems and urban modeling, architecture and building information modeling, medical imaging and simulation (mesh generation from images), computer graphics and visual effects, computer vision and photogrammetry (surface reconstruction from point clouds), robotics and motion planning, molecular modeling, and scientific computing (meshes for finite element simulations).

The page Projects Using CGAL gathers open source projects and publications that rely on CGAL, and GeometryFactory maintains a list of commercial customers.

How do I cite CGAL in a publication?

Please cite the specific packages you use, as well as the CGAL User and Reference Manual. The page Acknowledging CGAL explains how, and provides ready-to-use BibTeX entries.

How can I contribute to CGAL?

Contributions are welcome, from bug reports and fixes to new packages. See Getting Involved in the CGAL Project and the description of the submission and review process. If you use AI tools, please also read the AI guidelines of the project.

What is CGAL's stance on AI?

The CGAL project has published guidelines for the use of AI tools in contributions. In short, AI assistance is accepted as a tool, but not as a replacement for development skills: contributors must understand, review, and test all the code they submit, which must follow the project's conventions and be compatible with the license of the package. The human submitter is the sole author of the contribution and remains fully responsible for it.

Licensing and Commercial Use

Under which licenses is CGAL distributed?

CGAL is distributed under a dual-license scheme. It is available free of charge under open source licenses: the foundational building blocks (such as the kernels and the support library) are released under the GNU LGPL v3+, and most higher-level algorithms and data structures under the GNU GPL v3+. Alternatively, CGAL components are available under a commercial license from GeometryFactory. The software is identical under both schemes; only the rights and obligations differ. See The CGAL License for details.

How do I know the license of a given package?

The license of each package is given in its entry of the Package Overview. For the exact terms, always refer to the LICENSE file of the source code distribution.

Can I use CGAL in a commercial or closed-source product?

Yes. You have two options:

  • Comply with the open source licenses. The LGPL parts can be used in proprietary software, provided that the modifications you make to these parts are made available under the LGPL. If you distribute software that uses GPL parts, you must distribute the source code of your software under the GPL.
  • Obtain a commercial license from GeometryFactory for the CGAL components you use. You can then integrate them in proprietary software, sold or used internally, without the obligations of the GPL.

If you are unsure which option applies to your situation, please contact GeometryFactory.

What does a commercial license include?

GeometryFactory licenses CGAL per component (package), with offers adapted to industrial development, industrial research, and academic research. For industrial development, the license is a one-time fee, independent of the number of developers in your company, and without royalties when you distribute your software. Optional maintenance and support contracts give access to new releases and bug fixes of your licensed components and to a privileged channel for technical questions. See the license options on the GeometryFactory website.

Can I get professional support, training, or custom development?

Yes. GeometryFactory offers maintenance and support contracts, consulting, training, and the development of geometric algorithms that are not yet in CGAL. See its services page.

Using CGAL

Do I still need to install CGAL?

Not really. Since version 5.0, CGAL is a header-only library: there is no CGAL library to configure, compile, or link. You only need the CGAL header files, obtained from a package manager, from a source archive, or from the GitHub repository, together with the required third-party dependencies, which are not all header-only. When you configure your program with CMake, CGAL is configured at the same time; if CMake does not find CGAL automatically, set the variable CGAL_DIR to the root directory of the CGAL sources.

The required and optional dependencies are listed in the manual page Compilers and Third Party Dependencies, and the section Header-only Usage gives all the details.

Which package managers is CGAL available on?

Package managers is the recommended way to get CGAL, as they also install the required third-party dependencies with compatible versions. Package managers are not maintained by the CGAL project and may not offer the latest release immediately. See the download page for your platform:

  • Windows: vcpkg install cgal;
  • macOS: brew install cgal (CGAL is also available on MacPorts);
  • Linux: for example, sudo apt-get install libcgal-dev on Debian, Ubuntu, and derivatives, or the package manager of your distribution.

You can also download the source archive of any release from the GitHub releases page.

Which compilers and C++ standard are required?

Since version 6.0, CGAL requires a C++ compiler supporting C++17 or later. The list of compilers that are continuously tested with the latest release is given in the section Supported Compilers of the manual. Older compilers might work, but no guarantee is provided.

How do I compile a program using CGAL?

CGAL uses CMake. A minimal CMakeLists.txt for a program using CGAL is:

cmake_minimum_required(VERSION 3.22)
project(my_project)

find_package(CGAL REQUIRED)

add_executable(my_program my_program.cpp)
target_link_libraries(my_program PRIVATE CGAL::CGAL)

Configure and build it in Release mode (see why):

cmake -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release .
make

CGAL also provides the script cgal_create_CMakeLists to generate such a file. See the manual pages Using CGAL on Unix, Using CGAL on Windows, and How to use CGAL with CMake.

What are concepts and models?

CGAL is a library of C++ class and function templates. The requirements on their template arguments are expressed by concepts. A concept is a set of requirements on a type: nested types, member functions, or free functions that must be available. A model of a concept is a type that fulfills these requirements.

For example, the convex hull functions are parameterized by a traits class, which must be a model of the concept ConvexHullTraits_2; every CGAL kernel is a model of this concept. Similarly, most algorithms of the Polygon Mesh Processing packages accept any model of the mesh concepts, such as FaceGraph: CGAL::Surface_mesh and CGAL::Polyhedron_3 are models, and you can make your own mesh data structure a model too. In the reference manual, the API of a class is mainly documented on the pages of the concepts it is a model of.

See the section Concepts and Models of the Hello World tutorial and the organization of the reference manual.

Where can I find examples and demo programs?

The user manual of each package contains commented examples, whose source code is included in the manual; the tutorials are a good starting point. All examples and demos are also available in a separate archive, CGAL-x.y-examples.tar.xz, attached to each release on the GitHub releases page, as they are not installed by most package managers. On Debian and Ubuntu, they are provided by the package libcgal-demo, in /usr/share/doc/libcgal-demo/. Examples and demos are configured and built with CMake, as any other program using CGAL.

Can I use CGAL from Python?

Yes. The CGAL project maintains Python bindings covering a subset of the library, available on PyPI: 

pip install cgal

See the wiki of the bindings for the list of available packages and examples. The full functionality of CGAL is available in C++.

Which file formats can CGAL read and write?

CGAL reads and writes common file formats for meshes and point sets, such as OFF, OBJ, STL, PLY, and XYZ, as well as formats used in geographic information systems such as WKT. The complete list, with the corresponding functions, is given in the section Importing and Exporting Data using Standard File Formats of the manual. Some formats require optional dependencies.

Which version of CGAL should I use, and where do I find old releases?

We recommend the latest stable release, announced on the home page and listed on the Releases page together with its release notes. Patch releases (e.g., 6.2.1) only contain bug fixes. Before upgrading to a new major version, check the changelog for breaking changes.

All releases, including old ones, are available in the releases section of the CGAL GitHub repository. The manuals of past releases are available at https://doc.cgal.org/version/Manual/.

Robustness, Kernels, and Number Types

What does "exact computation" mean, and why does it matter?

Geometric algorithms take decisions based on tests such as "is this point to the left of this line?". When these tests are evaluated with floating-point arithmetic, roundoff errors can lead to inconsistent decisions, and then to wrong results, infinite loops, or crashes, typically on degenerate inputs (collinear points, coplanar faces, ...). CGAL follows the exact geometric computation paradigm: its kernels guarantee that these decisions are correct, while remaining fast thanks to arithmetic filtering. See The Exact Computation Paradigm.

CGAL offers several kernels. Which is the right one for my program?

In most cases, one of the predefined kernels is the right choice:

  • CGAL::Exact_predicates_inexact_constructions_kernel: exact predicates, constructions computed with double. This is the fastest robust choice, and it is sufficient for algorithms that only test properties of their input, such as most triangulations and convex hulls.
  • CGAL::Exact_predicates_exact_constructions_kernel: exact predicates and exact constructions. Use it when your program constructs new objects (intersection points, for example) that are then used in further computations, such as in Boolean operations or arrangements.
  • CGAL::Exact_predicates_exact_constructions_kernel_with_sqrt: additionally supports exact square roots.

The section Choosing a Kernel and Predefined Kernels of the kernel manual gives more details, including on the choice of number types. The user manual of each package also indicates which kernel is recommended for its algorithms.

What is the difference between a predicate and a construction, and why does it matter for robustness?

Geometric predicates test properties of geometric objects and return, for example, a Boolean value or an orientation: testing whether a point lies inside a sphere, or whether three points form a left turn, a right turn, or are collinear, are predicates. A geometric construction creates a new geometric object, such as the line through two points or the center of a circle defined by three points.

Constructions are problematic with respect to robustness, since the constructed object has to be stored in a particular representation. If this representation does not capture the object's properties exactly, due for example to roundoff errors, the correctness of subsequent computations with this object cannot be guaranteed. Exact constructions are more expensive than exact predicates, which is why the kernel CGAL::Exact_predicates_inexact_constructions_kernel exists.

I want to modify the coordinates of a point, but the CGAL kernel does not seem to support that. Why?

The kernel concept is representation-independent: there is no assumption on how a Point_3, for example, is represented. In particular, it need not be represented by Cartesian coordinates, so code relying on modifying these coordinates could be inefficient or wrong. CGAL algorithms are designed around predicates and constructions, which do not require this mutability. Non-modifiability also simplifies the reference-counting scheme used by default to copy CGAL objects.

To change a point, construct a new one and assign it: p = Point_2(p.x(), p.y() + 1);

How do I convert a number type, e.g. FT, to double?

All number types used by CGAL provide the conversion function CGAL::to_double(). For example: double x = CGAL::to_double(p.x());.

Polygons

How can I compute Boolean operations (union, intersection, difference) on polygons?

  • For polygons with or without holes, use the package 2D Regularized Boolean Set-Operations, which provides union, intersection, difference, symmetric difference, and complement, as well as intersection and containment predicates. It also supports polygons bounded by curves, such as circular arcs.
  • For non-regularized operations, which keep lower-dimensional features such as isolated edges and vertices and distinguish between the interior and the boundary, use 2D Boolean Operations on Nef Polygons.

For Boolean operations on 3D meshes, see below.

How do I triangulate the interior of a polygon?

Insert the edges of the polygon (and of its holes) as constraints into a constrained triangulation, such as CGAL::Constrained_Delaunay_triangulation_2. The result is a triangulation of the convex hull of the input in which the polygon edges are constrained edges. Then call CGAL::mark_domain_in_triangulation() to mark the faces that are inside the polygon. See the 2D Triangulations user manual, which contains an example.

How do I compute the offset of a polygon?

  • For offsets with mitered (sharp) corners, inward or outward, use the functions CGAL::create_interior_skeleton_and_offset_polygons_2() and CGAL::create_exterior_skeleton_and_offset_polygons_2() of the package 2D Straight Skeleton and Polygon Offsetting, which also handle polygons with holes and weighted offsets.
  • For offsets with rounded corners, use CGAL::approximated_offset_2() or the exact CGAL::offset_polygon_2() of the package 2D Minkowski Sums.

How do I repair an invalid polygon?

Polygons coming from real data often have self-intersections, overlapping edges, or incorrectly nested holes. The function CGAL::Polygon_repair::repair() of the package 2D Polygon Repair converts them into valid polygons with holes, following a choice of rules (e.g., even-odd or non-zero winding).

Arrangements

Is the outer boundary of a face of an arrangement a simple polygon?

Not necessarily. The outer CCB (connected component of the boundary) of a face of an arrangement may contain "inner antennas". Consider a face whose boundary is a simple polygon, with a vertex v on this boundary, and add a component that starts and ends at v and lies in the interior of the face (one edge incident to v suffices). The outer CCB of the face then traverses this edge twice.

Note also that with an inexact number type, you might get a non-simple polygon even if the outer CCB represents one, due to numerical errors.

How can I accelerate the work with arrangements?

First, make sure that your program is compiled in Release mode. Then:

  1. Passing x-monotone curves that are pairwise disjoint in their interior to the insertion functions is more efficient (in running time) and less demanding (in traits-class functionality) than passing general curves.
  2. When the curves to be inserted are segments that are pairwise disjoint in their interior, it is more efficient to use the traits class Arr_non_caching_segment_traits_2 rather than the default one, Arr_segment_traits_2.
  3. Prior knowledge of the combinatorial structure of the arrangement can be used to accelerate the insertion of x-monotone curves whose interior is disjoint from existing edges and vertices: the specialized functions insert_in_face_interior(), insert_from_left_vertex(), insert_from_right_vertex(), and insert_at_vertices() hardly involve any geometric operation. For example, a polygon can be inserted into an empty arrangement by inserting its first segment with insert_in_face_interior(), the following ones with insert_from_left_vertex() or insert_from_right_vertex(), and the last one with insert_at_vertices().
  4. The main trade-off among point-location strategies is between time and storage. The naive and walk strategies require no preprocessing and no auxiliary structures, but answer queries more slowly; they are preferable when point-location queries are rare compared to modifications of the arrangement.
  5. The traits classes should be instantiated with an exact kernel to ensure robustness when the input may be degenerate.
  6. Geometric operations dominate the running time of most operations: avoid repeating them, for example by storing and reusing their results.

See the 2D Arrangements user manual for details.

How can I attach information to a vertex, a halfedge, or a face of an arrangement?

Extend the DCEL (doubly connected edge list) of the arrangement, so that its vertex, halfedge, and face types contain the information you need. The package provides class templates that make this easy; see the section Extending the DCEL of the 2D Arrangements user manual. If only faces need to be extended, see the example examples/Arrangement_on_surface_2/face_extension.cpp; the example examples/Arrangement_on_surface_2/dcel_extension.cpp extends all feature types simultaneously.

Polygon Mesh Processing

CGAL provides a comprehensive set of algorithms for surface meshes, organized in the packages Polygon Mesh Processing, Boolean Operations on Meshes, Polygon Mesh Repair, and Meshing and Remeshing of Polygon Meshes. All functions are in the namespace CGAL::Polygon_mesh_processing.

Which data structure should I use for my surface mesh?

The mesh processing algorithms are generic: they work with any model of the mesh concepts. For new code, we recommend CGAL::Surface_mesh, an efficient halfedge data structure with dynamic properties. CGAL::Polyhedron_3 is also supported. If your application already has its own mesh data structure, you can use the algorithms directly on it, without copying, by providing the graph traits for your type.

How can I compute Boolean operations (union, intersection, difference) on meshes?

Use the functions corefine_and_compute_union(), corefine_and_compute_intersection(), and corefine_and_compute_difference() of the package Boolean Operations on Meshes. They work on triangle meshes that bound volumes and are fast and robust, including in degenerate configurations such as coplanar faces. The same package also provides clipping and splitting of meshes by a plane, a box, or another mesh (clip(), split()), and the computation of intersection polylines. For input meshes with self-intersections, see autorefine_triangle_soup().

For non-regularized Boolean operations on general polyhedra, see 3D Boolean Operations on Nef Polyhedra.

How do I repair a mesh or a polygon soup?

The package Polygon Mesh Repair provides tools for the most common defects:

  • to turn a polygon soup (for example, read from an STL file) into a mesh: repair_polygon_soup(), orient_polygon_soup(), and polygon_soup_to_polygon_mesh();
  • to merge duplicated boundaries: stitch_borders();
  • to fill holes: triangulate_refine_and_fair_hole() and related functions;
  • to fix orientation, degenerate faces, and non-manifold vertices, with dedicated functions.

Self-intersections can be detected with does_self_intersect() and self_intersections(). When the input is too damaged to be repaired, for example for raw scans or CAD models with many defects, 3D Alpha Wrapping generates a valid, watertight, and orientable surface mesh that encloses the input.

How do I remesh, simplify, or smooth a mesh?

  • Isotropic remeshing, with feature preservation: isotropic_remeshing(), in the package Meshing and Remeshing of Polygon Meshes, which also provides refinement, adaptive remeshing, and smoothing (angle_and_area_smoothing(), smooth_shape()).
  • Simplification by edge collapse: the package Triangulated Surface Mesh Simplification.

How do I compute the area, the volume, or the distance between meshes?

Use area(), volume(), and the other functions of the measure group, as well as approximate_Hausdorff_distance() to compare two meshes. See the Polygon Mesh Processing manual. For fast intersection and distance queries between a mesh and points, rays, or segments, see the AABB tree package.

How do I apply a transformation (translation, rotation, scaling) to a mesh?

Use CGAL::Polygon_mesh_processing::transform(), which applies a transformation, such as CGAL::Aff_transformation_3, to all the points of a mesh. See the Polygon Mesh Processing manual.

How do I compute the normals of a mesh?

Use compute_face_normal() for a single face, or compute_face_normals() and compute_vertex_normals() to compute the normals of all faces or vertices at once. See the Polygon Mesh Processing manual. Note that the plane() member function of the facets of CGAL::Polyhedron_3 does not compute the plane equation: it only gives access to a plane stored in the facet, if any.

Issues

How do I report an issue or get help?

  • Questions on how to use CGAL: ask on the user mailing list cgal-discuss or on the other channels listed on the page Mailing Lists and Forums. Search the archives first: your question may already have been answered.
  • Bugs, crashes, and wrong results: open an issue on the CGAL issue tracker, following the bug reporting instructions.
  • Professional support: see above.

In all cases, a small, self-contained program and input data reproducing the problem, together with the versions of CGAL, of the compiler, and of the operating system, greatly help others to answer quickly.

I think I have encountered a bug in CGAL. What should I do?

First, check that you are using the latest release, that your program is compiled in Release mode if the issue is about performance, and that you use an appropriate kernel: many apparent bugs are robustness issues. Then follow the bug reporting instructions and open an issue on GitHub.

My program is very slow. Why?

The most common reason is that the program was compiled in Debug mode. CGAL makes heavy use of C++ templates and contains many internal checks (assertions) that are enabled in Debug mode, which can make programs dramatically slower. Always measure performance in Release mode, for example with -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release when using CMake, which also defines NDEBUG and disables the assertions.

The choice of the kernel also has a large impact on performance: use the fastest kernel that provides the guarantees your program needs.

I am using double (or float) and get assertion failures or incorrect output. Is this a bug in CGAL?

While it may be a bug in CGAL, you are more likely facing a robustness problem. Using a kernel such as CGAL::Simple_cartesian<double> or CGAL::Cartesian<double> means that all predicates and constructions are evaluated with floating-point arithmetic, without any guarantee: small roundoff errors can have large consequences, up to the point where an algorithm crashes.

Use one of the predefined kernels instead: they let you keep double coordinates in your input and output, while guaranteeing that all decisions taken by the algorithms are correct. If your program constructs new objects that are used in further computations, use CGAL::Exact_predicates_exact_constructions_kernel. See also The Exact Computation Paradigm.

Good references that discuss the robustness problem in geometric computing are:

  • S. Schirra. Robustness and precision issues in geometric computation. In J.-R. Sack and J. Urrutia (eds.), Handbook of Computational Geometry, Chapter 14, Elsevier Science, 1999.
  • L. Kettner, K. Mehlhorn, S. Pion, S. Schirra, and C. Yap. Classroom Examples of Robustness Problems in Geometric Computations. Computational Geometry: Theory and Applications, 40(1):61-78, 2008. [HAL]

My debugger reports a CGAL::Uncertain_conversion_exception. Why?

This is an internal exception that is thrown and caught by CGAL as part of its arithmetic filtering mechanism. It is correct behavior and you can safely ignore it. Some debuggers report it as a "first-chance exception"; you can configure your debugger to ignore this exception type.

My programs take a long time to compile. What can I do?

CGAL makes heavy use of C++ templates, which has an impact on compilation time. The following hints can help:

  • Remove debugging options such as -g when you do not need them: generating debug information for template instantiations is costly in time and memory.
  • Remove optimization options such as -O2 in the early steps of the development cycle, when you do not need fast executables.
  • Regroup translation units: if your program is composed of many small translation units, reducing their number avoids parsing the same headers and instantiating the same templates many times.
  • Use precompiled headers: regroup the CGAL headers you use in one header and precompile it, for example with the CMake command target_precompile_headers().

I need a feature that CGAL does not offer. What can I do?

You can submit a feature request on the issue tracker, and check the page Work in Progress to see whether it is already planned. If you need the feature in a given time frame, GeometryFactory offers custom development.
